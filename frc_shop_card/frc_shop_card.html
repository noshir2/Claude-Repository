Safety first

This card is not a substitute for your team's safety training. It is the list a new mentor is asked about most.

    Breaker or fuse rating to minimum wire gauge

    Why this is not the house-wiring table

    If you have ever opened the NEC ampacity tables, these gauges look reckless: 12 AWG on a 40 A breaker, 18 AWG on 20 A. Four things are different on a robot. The runs are short and in free air, not bundled in conduit. The loads are intermittent: a drive motor pulls its peak for a second, not for three hours. The breakers are snap-action and trip fast on a real overload. And it is 12 V: at 12 V the thing that actually limits you is voltage drop, not insulation heating. A volt lost in a thin motor lead is 8% of your bus, and the brown-out threshold is only a few volts below where a tired battery already sits under load. So the manual's table is a minimum, and most teams go one or two gauges heavier on anything that pulls real current.

    The 'other rules' table on the Reference tab has the rest of the power section (battery, charger, roboRIO and radio supply, legal breakers and fuses, branch-circuit maxima, wire colours).

    Reference

    Copper wire resistance (AWG 6-24)

    Generated from the AWG formula and copper resistivity at 20 C, solid wire. Stranded wire of the same gauge reads about 2-5% higher. NEC Chapter 9 Table 8 lists resistance at 75 C for stranded conductors, so it reads about 22% higher than this table (copper's temperature coefficient is about 0.393% per degree C, times 55 degrees) plus the stranding allowance. For a robot wire at room temperature these are the right numbers. The ampacity columns are rules of thumb only; the rule table decides what you may fit.
